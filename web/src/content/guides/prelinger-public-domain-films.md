## Why so much old film is public domain

In the United States, works published in 1930 or earlier are now in the public domain. Many later films joined them because, under the law before 1978, copyright had to be claimed with a notice and renewed after 28 years; films published without a notice, or never renewed, lost their protection. Industrial, educational and sponsored films were often made for a single client and never renewed, which is why so many of them are free to use today. Works made by the US government, including many wartime and public-information films, were never copyrighted at all.

## The Prelinger Archives

The Prelinger Archives are a collection of "ephemeral" films (industrial, educational, advertising and amateur films) assembled by the archivist Rick Prelinger. Around ten thousand of them are on the Internet Archive, covering roughly 1903 to the 1980s: assembly lines and kitchens, cars and highways, schools and civil defence, cities and suburbs. Because the collection itself states that these films are in the public domain, Video Assets Server treats their licence as stated by the source.

## Everything else on the Internet Archive

Anyone can upload a film to the Internet Archive and pick its licence, and the Archive doesn't verify it. Most uploads are honest, but some modern films and TV recordings carry a public-domain mark they shouldn't. Video Assets Server only searches items whose licence is CC0, public domain or CC BY(-SA), leaves out TV captures, trailers and fan uploads, and caps uploader-chosen licences at **Unclear: check the terms**. Before you rely on one, check the item's description for the original producer and year, and whether the film plausibly entered the public domain.

## People and brands in old film

Copyright is not the only right in a film. The people in a 1950s newsreel or home movie never signed a model release, and the brands on its shop fronts are still trademarks. That is why archive footage carries the **editorial** flag: perfect for documentaries, essays and history channels, risky for an ad that uses a stranger's face to sell something.

## Working with archive footage in a modern edit

- Expect standard-definition transfers, 4:3 frames, film grain, flicker and black-and-white. Pillarbox rather than stretch.
- Films start with titles and countdown leaders: skip in, or let the [sequence builder](/sequence) set the in-point.
- Frame rates vary (18, 24, 25 or 29.97 fps transfers); conform them to your timeline.
- Download the MP4 derivative for editing; the original files can be large or in older formats.
