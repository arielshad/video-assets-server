## Why NASA footage is (mostly) free

Works made by US federal employees as part of their jobs are not protected by copyright in the US. NASA's own photos and video fall under that rule. NASA's media guidelines say its material is generally not copyrighted, may be used for educational and informational purposes, and may be used commercially as long as the use doesn't suggest NASA's endorsement. That makes NASA one of the largest sources of free, high-quality footage: about nine thousand videos in the Image and Video Library, from launches and spacewalks to Earth seen from the International Space Station, and about ten thousand visualisation pages from the Scientific Visualization Studio.

## The rules that still apply

- **Credit NASA.** NASA asks to be acknowledged as the source. Use the item's credit line when it has one (for example "NASA/Bill Ingalls" or "NASA/Goddard").
- **No endorsement.** Don't use NASA footage in a way that suggests NASA endorses you, your product, your channel or your opinions.
- **No insignia or logotype.** The NASA "meatball" insignia and the "worm" logotype are protected and not for general use. Footage that shows them on a rocket or a suit is fine in an explainer; using them as branding is not.
- **People.** Identifiable astronauts and NASA employees have rights in their likeness: NASA asks for permission before they appear in advertising.
- **Third-party material.** Some footage on NASA's sites was made by partners: launch companies, universities, other space agencies. If the credit line names another organisation, that organisation may own the copyright.
- **AI wording.** NASA's guidelines also cover AI tools: their output shouldn't be presented as "according to NASA".

## How it shows up here

Every NASA clip is **Commercial use OK with credit**, carries a ready credit line and has the **editorial** flag, which stands for the endorsement, insignia and astronaut rules above. Clips whose credit names an outside organisation are **Unclear: check the terms**. Visualisations from the Scientific Visualization Studio have no people or insignia in them, so they are not flagged editorial.

NASA videos come as a set of MP4 files: the original master (often 1080p or 4K) and smaller renditions down to a tiny preview. Pick the rendition that matches your timeline rather than the master, which can be large.

## Outside the US

NASA states that its material is generally not copyrighted in the United States. Other countries can treat US government works differently. In practice NASA footage is used worldwide, but if you publish primarily under another country's law and the stakes are high, check how it treats foreign government works.
